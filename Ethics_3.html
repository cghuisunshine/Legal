Key point: IT is not considered a "mature" or "formal" profession like engineering, medicine, or law. However, standards and expectations of professional conduct still apply.

A18. Trade Secret

A trade secret is confidential business information that provides a competitive advantage.

  • Is it clear what's protected? No – because it's a secret
  • Do you have to apply? No – but you must keep it secret
  • How long does protection last? Permanent – as long as it remains secret

Example: The Google PageRank algorithm (current protection)

A19. Trademark

A trademark is a sign, symbol, word, or phrase that identifies and distinguishes goods or services.

  • Is it clear what's protected? Yes – especially if registered
  • Do you have to apply? No – but registration aids protection
  • How long does protection last? 10 years, renewable in Canada

Examples: The name "BCIT," the McDonald's jingle, company logos

A20. Copyright

Copyright protects original literary, artistic, musical, and dramatic works.

  • Is it clear what's protected? Yes – especially if registered
  • Do you have to apply? No – but registration aids protection
  • How long does protection last? Lifetime of the author + 70 years after death

Examples: A screenplay, music, software code, books

A21. Patent

A patent protects inventions and grants the holder exclusive rights to make, use, or sell the invention.

  • Is it clear what's protected? Yes – the patent is open and documented
  • Do you have to apply? Yes – application is required
  • How long does protection last? Up to 20 years, not renewable

Examples: Ozempic (pharmaceutical), documented inventions on CIPO/USPTO websites

A22. Open Source

Open source refers to software or content that can be freely copied, reproduced, altered, and distributed by anyone.

  • Is it clear what's protected? Yes – but it's intentionally free to use
  • Do you have to apply? No
  • Protection duration: Not applicable – designed for open access

Example: The Linux operating system

A23. Traditional Knowledge (TK) and IP Law Problems

Traditional Knowledge (TK) refers to the knowledge, innovations, and practices of Indigenous peoples, developed and maintained over generations.

Why it's problematic under standard IP law:

  1. Collective ownership not recognized: TK is owned collectively by Indigenous Nations, but the IPR system does not acknowledge collective ownership
  2. Public domain problem: Under IPR, knowledge not "protected" is in the public domain. Since Indigenous Peoples have not used IPR to protect their knowledge, TK is often treated as public domain without regard for Customary Law
  3. Authorship problem: A person who "writes down" TK or Oral Traditions is not their author – the copyright should belong to the Indigenous People, not the person who recorded it
  4. Perpetual protection: Customary Law dictates that certain TK is never intended for external access, but IPR assumes all IP will eventually enter the public domain

Quote from slides: "Native culture was thought to be free for the taking, the product of many and so the preserve of none – except when transformed by the mediation of Europeans, whereupon it magically gained cultural legitimacy."

Part B – Additional Longer Questions Answer Key

A24. Data Breach and ACM Code of Ethics

The ACM Code of Ethics provides guidance for computing professionals. Relevant principles include:

  1. Principle 1.2: Avoid harm – Computing professionals should avoid causing harm to others. Failing to report or address a data breach causes harm to customers whose data is exposed.

  2. Principle 1.6: Respect privacy – Professionals have an obligation to protect personal information. An unencrypted data breach violates this principle.

  3. Principle 2.5: Give comprehensive evaluations – Professionals should provide honest assessments of system capabilities, limitations, and risks.

  4. Principle 3.7: Recognize responsibility to report – If a professional believes a system poses risks to stakeholders, they have an obligation to report concerns.

Conclusion: The developer should:

  • Report the breach through appropriate channels
  • Advocate for notifying affected customers
  • Work to remediate the vulnerability
  • Document the incident for future prevention

The ACM Code applies not just to ACM members but serves as a general standard for computing professionals.

A25. Traditional Knowledge vs. Standard IPR

Aspect Canadian IPR System Indigenous Customary Law
Ownership Individual or corporate Collective (belonging to Indigenous Nations)
Registration Required for full protection No registration – protection through tradition
Duration Limited terms (20 years for patents, life + 70 for copyright) Perpetual – some knowledge never meant for external access
Public domain All IP eventually enters public domain Some TK must remain restricted permanently
Authorship Person who creates/writes owns Community owns regardless of who records it

The "Public Domain Problem":

Under IPR, knowledge that isn't formally protected is considered public domain – free for anyone to use. This creates two major problems:

  1. Since Indigenous Peoples historically did not use IPR mechanisms, their TK is often treated as public domain by outsiders
  2. The IPR assumption that all IP should eventually enter public domain conflicts with Customary Law, which dictates that certain sacred or sensitive knowledge must never be shared externally

This has led to widespread misappropriation, with thousands of patents on TK licensed to corporations and individuals worldwide.

A26. Indigenous Petroglyphs and Trademark Protection

The Snuneymuxw Case:

  • Snuneymuxw People have ancient petroglyphs near False Narrows on Gabriola Island, BC
  • In the early 1990s, non-Indigenous businesses began using petroglyph images in logos
  • In 1998, the Snuneymuxw Band was awarded a Section 9(1)(n) Public Authority Trademark for eight petroglyphs
  • This protects the images from all uses by non-Snuneymuxw people
  • Businesses using the images were forced to stop

Why using Indigenous imagery is problematic:

  1. Legal: Without permission, using protected Indigenous symbols may violate trademark law
  2. Ethical: Even without formal legal protection, using cultural symbols without consent treats Indigenous culture as "free for the taking"
  3. Harm: Commercial use of sacred or culturally significant images can cause harm to Indigenous communities, violating utilitarian principles
  4. Respect for autonomy: Kantian ethics would require treating Indigenous Peoples as ends in themselves – respecting their right to control their cultural property

Conclusion: Beyond legal requirements, ethical considerations demand seeking consent and understanding the cultural significance of Indigenous symbols before any commercial or public use.
