Q16. Case Study — The Trolley Problem & Autonomous Vehicles

An autonomous vehicle (AV) is travelling at highway speed. A sudden brake failure means the car cannot stop. It must choose between:

  • Option A: Continue straight, striking 3 pedestrians who crossed illegally
  • Option B: Swerve, striking 1 pedestrian on the sidewalk who did nothing wrong

The AV's software must be programmed with a decision rule in advance.

a) Who are the stakeholders in this decision?

b) Apply Act Utilitarianism to determine which option the AV should be programmed to choose. Show your reasoning.

c) Apply Kantianism to the same scenario. Does it reach the same conclusion? Why or why not?

d) What does this scenario reveal about the limits of using a single ethical theory to make technology decisions?

Part A – Answer Key

A1. Society A society is an association of people organized under a system of rules designed to advance the good of its members over time. (Trevor's exact slide definition)

A2. Morality vs. Ethics Morality refers to a society's rules of conduct — what people ought and ought not to do in various situations. Ethics is the philosophical study of morality — a rational examination of those moral beliefs and behaviors. The difference: morality is the set of rules; ethics is the reasoned analysis of those rules.

A3. Ethics Ethics is the philosophical study of morality — a rational examination into people's moral beliefs and behavior. (Trevor's exact slide definition)

A4. Workable Ethical Theory A workable ethical theory must allow you to examine moral problems, reach conclusions logically, and defend those conclusions in front of a skeptical, yet open-minded audience. (This is Trevor's exact definition from the Terms slide — memorize this phrasing.)

A5. Relativism Relativism is the view that moral judgements are not universal — what is right or wrong depends on the person or group making the judgement. The two forms are:

  • Subjective Relativism: Each individual decides for themselves what is right
  • Cultural Relativism: What is right is determined by one's culture or society

A6. Ethical Egoism Ethical egoism holds that the morally right action is whatever best serves one's own long-term self-interest. It is not simply selfishness — it is a philosophical position that self-interest should be the basis of moral decisions.

A7. Divine Command Theory Divine Command Theory holds that an action is morally right if and only if God commands it, and morally wrong if God forbids it. Morality is derived from religious authority.

A8. Why These Are Unworkable All four fail the workable theory test: they cannot be used to logically examine moral problems and defend conclusions to a skeptical audience.

  • Subjective Relativism: Makes moral disagreement impossible — if everyone is right for themselves, there's no basis to say anyone is wrong
  • Cultural Relativism: Cannot critique other cultures; leads to moral isolationism
  • Divine Command Theory: Cannot be verified; different religious texts conflict; provides no mechanism for rational debate
  • Ethical Egoism: Leads to social conflict and cannot generate cooperative rules; if everyone acts only in self-interest, society breaks down

A9. Principle of Utility The Principle of Utility states that we should act so as to produce the greatest good (happiness/benefit) for the greatest number of people affected by the action. It is the foundation of Utilitarian ethics.

A10. Consequentialism Consequentialism means that the moral value of an action is determined entirely by its outcomes or consequences — not by the intention behind it, or by a rule or duty. Act Utilitarianism is consequentialist because it judges each individual act solely by whether it produces more benefit than harm.

A11. Good / Bad Actions in Act Utilitarianism

  • An action is good if it produces more happiness/benefit than harm across all affected parties
  • An action is bad if it produces more harm than benefit, i.e. if an alternative action would have produced greater net benefit

A12. Act vs. Rule Utilitarianism

  • Act Utilitarianism: Evaluate each individual act case by case — choose the act that maximizes utility in this specific situation
  • Rule Utilitarianism: Follow general rules that, if widely adopted, tend to maximize utility overall. You don't re-calculate for every situation; you follow the rule that generally produces the best outcomes
  • Key distinction: Rule Utilitarianism is more consistent and predictable; Act Utilitarianism is more flexible but can justify harmful individual acts if the numbers work out

Part B – Answer Key (Model Answers)

A13. AI Chatbot Scenario

Using Act Utilitarianism: The core principle is the Principle of Utility — the right action produces the greatest benefit for the greatest number.

Stakeholders: you, your manager, clients, the company.

Benefits of using AI secretly: faster resolutions, better performance metrics, more time for development work. Harms: clients may receive lower-quality responses; your manager is deceived; if discovered, trust is broken; company reputation may suffer.

Conclusion using Act Util: Probably no — the deception introduces risks (discovery, trust breakdown, client harm) that likely outweigh short-term efficiency gains. However, if you disclosed AI use to your manager, the analysis changes — benefits may then outweigh harms.

Weakness of Act Utilitarianism: it can justify deception whenever the numbers favour it, which feels uncomfortable — this is why Rule Utilitarianism or Kantianism might be more appropriate here (a rule like "always disclose AI use" produces better outcomes generally, even if the individual calculation might sometimes favour hiding it).

A14. Highway Routing Scenario

Act Utilitarian Analysis: Costs: $20M (homeowner compensation) + $10M (construction) + $1M (wildlife habitat) = $31M Benefits: $39M (savings in automobile driving costs) Net benefit: $8M — benefits exceed costs → building the highway is the right action.

Two missing elements from the analysis:

  1. Non-monetary harms to the 150 displaced families (emotional distress, community disruption, loss of home) are not captured in the $20M compensation figure
  2. Long-term environmental harms beyond the initial $1M estimate (ecosystem damage, species loss, climate impact) are likely undervalued (Other valid answers: future maintenance costs, quality-of-life impacts, distribution of benefits vs. who bears the costs)

Kantian Comparison: A Kantian analysis would likely reach a different conclusion. The Categorical Imperative asks: could we universalize the principle "governments may demolish people's homes when it produces net economic benefit"? Most would say no — this treats homeowners purely as means to an end (economic efficiency), not as ends in themselves. Kant would likely say the project is impermissible regardless of the cost-benefit outcome.

A15. Comparing Two Unworkable Theories

Example: Subjective Relativism vs. Ethical Egoism

Subjective Relativism:

  • Core claim: Each person determines what is right for themselves
  • Case for: Respects individual autonomy; avoids cultural imperialism
  • Why it fails: Makes moral disagreement impossible and provides no grounds for criticizing any behaviour — a serial killer would be "right" by their own standards

Ethical Egoism:

  • Core claim: The morally right action is whatever serves your long-term self-interest
  • Case for: Aligns with rational self-preservation; encourages productivity and personal responsibility
  • Why it fails: In a society of ethical egoists, cooperation breaks down; it cannot generate shared moral rules or resolve conflicts between competing self-interests

Part C – Case Study Answer Key

A16. Trolley Problem / Autonomous Vehicle

a) Stakeholders:

  • The 3 pedestrians who crossed illegally
  • The 1 pedestrian on the sidewalk
  • Passengers in the AV
  • The AV manufacturer
  • Regulators and the public (who must live with whatever rule is programmed)
  • Future pedestrians and drivers affected by the policy

b) Act Utilitarian Analysis: Maximize total welfare across all parties.

  • Option A: 3 people harmed
  • Option B: 1 person harmed

Applying the Principle of Utility: Option B (swerve, harm 1) produces less total harm → the AV should be programmed to swerve. Note: a pure Act Utilitarian would say this regardless of fault or innocence — only the numbers matter.

c) Kantian Analysis: Kant's Categorical Imperative: act only according to principles you could universalize, and never treat persons merely as means.

Option B deliberately directs the car toward an innocent bystander, using that person as a means to save three others. A Kantian would likely say this is impermissible — you cannot intentionally harm an innocent person, even to save more lives. This is similar to the classic "fat man" variant of the trolley problem.

Conclusion: Kantianism may lead to Option A (do not swerve) — accepting the harm that results from inaction is different from causing harm to an innocent person deliberately.

d) What this reveals about ethical theories: No single theory produces a universally satisfying answer. Utilitarianism can endorse harming innocents when the numbers justify it; Kantianism can seem rigid when the stakes are life and death. This is exactly why IT professionals need to understand multiple ethical frameworks — real decisions (like programming AV behaviour) involve competing values that no single theory can resolve perfectly.
