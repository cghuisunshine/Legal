Weeks 2 & 3 – Introduction to Ethics

📖 Quinn Ch. 2 – Introduction to Ethics

What Makes an Ethical Theory "Workable"?

A workable theory must:

  1. Be applicable across different situations
  2. Produce consistent, justifiable results
  3. Not contradict widely-held moral intuitions without good reason

Unworkable Ethical Theories (Week 2)

Theory Core Idea Why Unworkable
Subjective Relativism Each person decides what's right for themselves No basis for moral disagreement or critique
Cultural Relativism What's right is defined by one's culture Can't critique other cultures; no universal standard
Divine Command Theory Right = what God commands Can't verify commands; moral dilemmas remain
Ethical Egoism Right = what's in my own best interest Leads to social conflict; no basis for cooperation

Workable Ethical Theories (Week 3)

Theory Core Idea Key Concept
Kantianism Act only on principles you'd will to be universal laws Categorical Imperative; duty-based (deontological)
Act Utilitarianism The right act maximizes total happiness/utility Principle of Utility; consequentialist
Rule Utilitarianism Follow rules that, if widely adopted, maximize utility Balances consistency with outcomes
Social Contract Theory Morality = rules rational people agree to for mutual benefit Rawls' veil of ignorance; justice as fairness
Virtue Ethics Focus on character traits (virtues) rather than acts What would a virtuous person do?

Utilitarianism – Key Concepts

  • Principle of Utility: An action is good if it produces the greatest good for the greatest number
  • Good action: Produces more happiness/benefit than harm overall
  • Bad action: Produces more harm than benefit overall
  • Consequentialist: Moral judgement is based on outcomes/consequences, not intentions or rules

Week 4 – Professional Ethics

📖 Quinn Ch. 9 – Professional Ethics

Is IT a "Mature Profession"?

IT is not considered a fully mature profession (yet). Compare to medicine or law.

8 Attributes of a Mature Profession

  1. Specialized knowledge/skills
  2. Formal education requirements
  3. Certification or licensing
  4. Professional associations
  5. Code of ethics
  6. Service orientation (public good)
  7. Autonomy / self-regulation
  8. Accountability mechanisms

Even Without "Mature" Status...

  • There can still be standards and expectations of professionalism
  • Companies create their own codes (e.g., Google Code of Conduct)

ACM Code of Ethics

  • Purpose: To guide ethical behaviour for computing professionals
  • Similarities to Google Code of Conduct: Public interest, honesty, privacy
  • Differences: ACM is universal/profession-wide; Google's is company-specific

Week 5 – Intellectual Property

📖 Quinn Ch. 4 – Intellectual Property

Four Types of IP

Type What it Protects Formal Application? Duration (Canada)
Trade Secret Confidential business info (e.g. formula, process) No – must just be kept secret As long as it remains secret
Trademark Brand names, logos, symbols Yes – must register to get full protection Renewable every 10 years (indefinite)
Copyright Original creative works (writing, code, music, art) No – automatic upon creation Life of author + 70 years
Patent Inventions, novel processes Yes – must apply and be granted 20 years from filing date

Open Source

  • Software whose source code is publicly available
  • Users can use, modify, and distribute it
  • Examples: Linux, Firefox, Android

Traditional Knowledge (TK) & IP

  • Collective ownership – TK is often held by a community, not an individual
  • Traditional Knowledge (TK) – Indigenous knowledge, practices, and expressions passed through generations
  • Why TK doesn't fit IP categories:
    • Copyright requires a named author (TK is communal and oral)
    • Patents require novelty and a filing date (TK predates modern systems)
    • Trade secrets require active secrecy (TK may be openly shared within community)
  • Key ethical questions:
    • Is something publicly accessible the same as ethically free to use? → No
    • Who holds copyright if someone writes down an oral tradition?
    • Should "prior art" include Indigenous knowledge even if not formally published?

Week 6 – Automation & Globalization

📖 Quinn Ch. 10 – Automation and Globalization

Utilitarian Analysis Framework (used in the debate)

Apply the Principle of Utility to automation/AI:

  1. Identify all affected parties
  2. List harms and benefits for each
  3. Weigh total harm vs. total benefit
  4. The ethical choice = the one with the greatest net benefit

Data Centres & AI – Case Study for Exam Prep

Issue: Data centres (especially those running LLMs) produce significant GHG emissions.

Details
Who benefits? Tech companies, consumers using AI/cloud services, researchers, businesses
Who is harmed? Communities near data centres (pollution, water use), future generations (climate change), people in regions most affected by climate change
Utilitarian verdict Depends on whether benefits (e.g. medical AI, productivity) outweigh environmental harms — context matters

Exam Tips

  • Definition questions: Be precise. Use course terminology. Don't confuse morality vs. ethics, or act vs. rule utilitarianism.
  • Longer questions: Structure your answer — state the theory, apply it to the scenario, draw a conclusion.
  • Case study: Identify the stakeholders, apply a workable ethical theory (Kantianism, Utilitarianism, or Social Contract), and justify your position.
  • You will have some choice on the longer questions — pick the theory you know best!
