In professional situations, I determine what is right or wrong by combining personal values, professional standards, and the potential impact of my decisions on others. I believe ethical awareness begins when a decision affects people — their privacy, safety, dignity, fairness, or well-being — not just technical outcomes. 2) “Unworkable” ethical theories
Definitions of morality/ethics/society, workable vs unworkable, persuasion without coercion.
INPUT OUTPUT
Input
Introduction This week we discussed "unworkable" ethical theories. Why are they "unworkable"? Why does that matter? Discussion Topic What are "morality", "ethics", and "society"? What does it mean for an ethical theory to be "workable" or "unworkable"? Do you personally subscribe to (i.e., believe in) any of those theories identified in the textbook as "unworkable"? Can anyone actually convince someone else - without using some form of coercion - to do, or not do something that has an ethical aspect to it?
Output
This week’s discussion about “unworkable” ethical theories helped me understand that not every moral idea can function as a practical guide for real-world decision-making. An ethical theory is considered “unworkable” when it fails to provide consistent guidance, cannot resolve disagreements, or leads to contradictions that make decision-making impossible. This matters because professionals need frameworks that actually help them make difficult choices under pressure, not theories that collapse when applied. **Morality** refers to the beliefs and values individuals hold about right and wrong. It is personal and often shaped by culture, religion, upbringing, and experience. **Ethics**, in contrast, is the systematic study of morality. It attempts to analyze, justify, and organize moral principles into coherent frameworks. Ethics is more structured and reflective. **Society** is the larger community within which shared norms and expectations develop. Society influences morality, and in turn, collective moral views shape laws and professional standards. An ethical theory is “workable” if it can be consistently applied, provides guidance in complex situations, and allows meaningful discussion across different perspectives. It is “unworkable” if it prevents dialogue (for example, if it claims morality is purely subjective and cannot be debated) or if it produces conclusions that are impractical or contradictory. Personally, I do not fully subscribe to ethical relativism or subjectivism — two theories often described as unworkable. If morality is entirely subjective (“right for me, wrong for you”), then professional standards lose their foundation. In fields like IT, engineering, or medicine, we need shared principles such as honesty, fairness, and responsibility. Without common standards, accountability becomes impossible. However, I do recognize that culture and context influence moral reasoning, so I think some awareness of relativism is useful — but not as a complete theory. Regarding persuasion, I believe people can influence others ethically without coercion through reason, dialogue, empathy, and shared values. While we cannot force someone to change their moral beliefs without coercion, we can appeal to consistency, consequences, fairness, or professional responsibility. Ethical growth often happens through conversation, not force. Coercion may produce compliance, but it does not produce moral understanding. Ultimately, ethical theories matter because they shape how we justify our decisions — especially in professional contexts where our choices affect others. A workable ethical theory helps us move beyond “that’s just my opinion” and toward reasoned, accountable decision-making.
3) Act Utilitarianism scenario
Definition + consequentialism + stakeholder/consequence analysis for data deletion vs retention.
INPUT OUTPUT
Input
Introduction This week we discussed "workable" ethical theories. One workable ethical theory is Act Utilitarianism. Discussion Topic This Discussion Topic this week is a little different than the usual format: Write a brief definition of Act Utilitarianism In particular, your definition must include an explanation of how Act Utilitarianism is used to arrive at ethical decisions, and explain what "consequentialist" means in this context. Analyze the scenario below using Act Utilitarianism: Identify the stakeholders and list 2-3 possible actions/choices available to the stakeholders. For each action, analyze: Who is affected (stakeholders)? What are the positive and negative consequences for each group? How significant are these consequences (i.e., short-term and long-term)? Weigh the consequences by comparing the total happiness/utility produced by each action. Identify which action results in the greatest net benefit (or least harm) for all affected. Scenario An IT company develops a popular mobile app that collects user data to improve its services. The company has the technical ability to retain user data indefinitely, which allows for better personalization and targeted advertising. However, some users are concerned about their privacy and want to be able to ask to have their data deleted on demand.
Output
## Brief Definition of Act Utilitarianism **Act Utilitarianism** is a consequentialist ethical theory that evaluates the morality of individual actions based on their outcomes. An action is morally right if, among the available alternatives, it produces the greatest overall happiness (or utility) for the greatest number of people affected. In this context, **“consequentialist”** means that the rightness or wrongness of an action depends entirely on its consequences, not on intentions, rules, or duties. Under Act Utilitarianism, each specific decision is evaluated case by case by comparing the total positive and negative consequences for all stakeholders. To arrive at an ethical decision using Act Utilitarianism: 1. Identify stakeholders. 2. Identify possible actions. 3. Evaluate the positive and negative consequences of each action. 4. Weigh the total utility (benefits minus harms). 5. Choose the action that produces the greatest net benefit overall. --- ## Scenario Analysis Using Act Utilitarianism ### Stakeholders - App users - The IT company (management, employees, shareholders) - Advertisers/business partners - Society at large (data privacy norms, digital trust) --- ### Possible Actions 1. **Retain user data indefinitely (no deletion option).** 2. **Allow users to request deletion of their data at any time.** 3. **Implement a limited retention policy (e.g., delete inactive data after a set period and allow optional deletion).** --- ## Action 1: Retain Data Indefinitely ### Positive Consequences - Company: Higher profits from targeted advertising. - Advertisers: More precise marketing. - Some users: Improved personalization and convenience. ### Negative Consequences - Users: Privacy risks, anxiety, potential data breaches. - Society: Weakening of privacy norms. - Long-term risk: Loss of user trust if misuse occurs. ### Significance Short-term: High financial benefit. Long-term: Potential severe harm if data is leaked or misused; erosion of trust. **Utility Assessment:** Financial gain is significant but concentrated in the company and advertisers. Privacy harm affects a larger group and carries long-term societal risk. Net utility is unstable and potentially negative long-term. --- ## Action 2: Allow On-Demand Data Deletion ### Positive Consequences - Users: Increased control, trust, privacy protection. - Society: Stronger digital ethics norms. - Company: Enhanced reputation and user loyalty long-term. ### Negative Consequences - Company: Reduced advertising efficiency and possible revenue loss. - Advertisers: Less precise targeting. ### Significance Short-term: Financial cost to company. Long-term: Increased trust, lower legal/regulatory risk, stronger brand. **Utility Assessment:** While profits may decrease somewhat, benefits (privacy protection, trust, reduced risk) are distributed across a larger number of people. Long-term utility likely higher. --- ## Action 3: Limited Retention + Optional Deletion ### Positive Consequences - Users: Reasonable privacy protection and choice. - Company: Retains enough data for business optimization. - Advertisers: Moderate targeting effectiveness. - Society: Balanced privacy norm. ### Negative Consequences - Some operational cost to implement deletion systems. - Slightly reduced advertising precision. ### Significance Short-term: Moderate adjustment cost. Long-term: Sustainable balance between profit and privacy. **Utility Assessment:** This option spreads benefits broadly while limiting harms. It reduces severe privacy risks while maintaining business viability. --- ## Weighing Total Utility - Action 1 maximizes profit but risks widespread long-term harm. - Action 2 strongly protects users but may significantly reduce business revenue. - Action 3 balances economic benefits and privacy protection. From an **Act Utilitarian** perspective, **Action 3 (limited retention with optional deletion)** likely produces the greatest net overall utility because it: - Protects user privacy, - Maintains business sustainability, - Reduces long-term risk, - Promotes social trust in technology. It generates substantial happiness while minimizing widespread harm. --- ## Conclusion Act Utilitarianism supports the action that produces the greatest total benefit for all affected stakeholders. In this case, a balanced data retention policy with user control creates the highest overall utility when both short-term and long-term consequences are considered.
4) Being a professional now (ACM)
Professional meaning, persona differences, and agreement that you’re a professional now.
INPUT OUTPUT
Input
Introduction You are an IT professional now. Not when you graduate. Not when you get paid. Now. That distinction comes with certain responsibilities. Discussion Topic What does it mean to be a "professional" in this industry? Is there, or can there be a difference between your professional persona and your persona in all other areas of your life? Do you agree or disagree with the assertion above that you are a professional now? Why, or why not? You can have a look at the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct for reference.
Output
To be a “professional” in the IT industry means more than having a job title or earning a salary. It means accepting responsibility for the impact of your technical decisions on others. Technology shapes communication, privacy, safety, access to information, and even democracy. A professional understands that their work has consequences beyond code efficiency or system performance. The **ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct** emphasizes principles such as contributing to society and human well-being, avoiding harm, being honest and trustworthy, respecting privacy, and honoring confidentiality. These are not skills that suddenly activate upon graduation. They apply the moment we begin designing systems, writing code, analyzing data, or advising others about technology. If I build an application today—even as a student—it can affect real users. That reality carries professional responsibility. I agree with the assertion that I am a professional now. Professionalism is about mindset and accountability, not employment status. If I represent myself as someone with technical knowledge, people may rely on my advice or my work. That reliance creates ethical obligations. For example, I cannot ignore security best practices or dismiss privacy concerns simply because I am “still learning.” Being in training does not remove responsibility; it increases the need for care and humility. Regarding professional persona versus personal persona, I think there can be differences in tone and boundaries, but not in core values. I may communicate more formally in a workplace than with friends, but honesty, fairness, and respect should remain consistent. If my ethical standards change depending on context, then my professionalism becomes performative rather than genuine. Ultimately, being a professional in IT means combining technical competence with ethical awareness. It means recognizing that trust is central to our field—and trust must be earned through consistent, principled behavior, both now and in the future.
5) IP rights and ethics
Clear position: there are cases where exclusive IP rights are unethical; examples + advice.
INPUT OUTPUT
Input
Introduction Are you looking to become the next Bill Gates or the next Linus Torvalds? Or maybe you could become the first example of a new type of legend? Intellectual property (IP) rights can be a contentious issue, especially when it comes to the ownership of IP that is generated through academic or government funding. On the one hand, many argue that IP is a valuable form of property that should be protected like any other asset, and that creators of IP should have exclusive rights to profit from their work. On the other hand, some argue that certain types of IP, such as medical research or software that is critical to public infrastructure, should be considered a public good and not subject to exclusive ownership. Discussion Topic What do you think? Should IP rights be protected like any other kind of property, or are there cases where it is unethical to have exclusive IP rights? Consider the role of academic and government funding in the creation of IP - does the source of funding have any bearing on who should own the resulting IP? How might different types of IP (e.g. patents, trademarks, copyrights) affect the ethical considerations surrounding ownership? Take a clear position, either (i) IP rights be protected like any other kind of property in all cases, or (ii) there are cases where it is unethical to have exclusive IP rights. In your response, be sure to provide specific examples of situations where the ownership of IP has been a contentious issue, and explain why you think the ethical considerations involved in these cases are important. Can you think of any potential drawbacks or risks to either protecting or limiting IP rights? What advice would you give to creators of IP - which might be you - who are grappling with these issues? For fun, and as an example, you might consider this recent, high-profile case of IP protections expiring.
Output
## Position I take the position that **there are cases where it is unethical to have exclusive IP rights**. While intellectual property should generally be protected to encourage innovation and reward creators, there are situations—especially those involving public funding, public health, or critical infrastructure—where strict exclusivity conflicts with broader social welfare. --- ## Why IP Protection Matters IP rights (patents, copyrights, trademarks) exist to incentivize creativity and innovation. Without protection, inventors and creators might lack financial motivation to invest time and resources into research and development. For example: - **Patents** encourage pharmaceutical innovation by granting temporary monopolies. - **Copyrights** protect authors, artists, and software developers. - **Trademarks** protect brand identity and consumer trust. These protections can generate economic growth and technological advancement. However, ethical issues arise when exclusive control harms the public. --- ## Case 1: Pharmaceutical Patents and Public Health A well-known example is the controversy over **HIV/AIDS medications in the early 2000s** and, more recently, COVID-19 vaccine patents. Pharmaceutical companies held patents that limited generic production in low-income countries. Critics argued that life-saving medicines—especially when developed with public or government funding—should not be restricted by exclusive rights that make them unaffordable. When taxpayer money funds research (e.g., university labs or government grants), it becomes ethically problematic if the resulting treatment is priced beyond public reach. In such cases, strict IP enforcement may protect corporate profit at the expense of human life. Here, the source of funding matters. If the public financed the innovation, there is a strong argument that the public should benefit directly. --- ## Case 2: Software and Public Infrastructure Another example is proprietary software used in public infrastructure (e.g., voting systems, healthcare systems). When the source code is fully proprietary and inaccessible, transparency and accountability may suffer. In some contexts, open-source models (like Linux) demonstrate how shared intellectual property can produce robust, secure, and widely beneficial systems. When software becomes critical to democratic processes or essential services, complete exclusivity can undermine public trust. --- ## Ethical Differences by Type of IP Different types of IP raise different concerns: - **Patents** (especially in medicine or green technology) can directly affect human survival and environmental sustainability. - **Copyrights** often raise concerns about access to education and knowledge (e.g., expensive academic journals). - **Trademarks** generally pose fewer ethical concerns because they protect branding rather than restricting access to life-saving knowledge. Thus, the ethical weight varies by context and impact. --- ## Risks of Limiting IP Rights Limiting IP protection carries risks: - Reduced incentive for innovation. - Decreased private investment in research. - Uncertainty for entrepreneurs and startups. If inventors cannot recover costs, especially in high-risk industries like pharmaceuticals, innovation could slow. --- ## Risks of Overprotecting IP Overly strict IP protection can: - Increase inequality. - Restrict access to essential goods. - Concentrate power in large corporations. - Slow scientific collaboration. Excessive monopolies can also stifle follow-on innovation. --- ## Advice to Creators (Including Ourselves) As future IT professionals and creators, we should: 1. Understand the social impact of our work. 2. Consider licensing models carefully (e.g., open-source vs. proprietary). 3. Reflect on whether our innovation serves primarily private profit or public benefit. 4. Recognize when collaboration may generate greater long-term impact than exclusivity. Ethical professionalism requires balancing personal reward with societal responsibility. --- ## Conclusion IP rights are important and often necessary. However, there are clear cases—particularly in publicly funded research, healthcare, and critical infrastructure—where strict exclusivity becomes ethically problematic. The challenge is not choosing between protection or openness in all cases, but determining when exclusivity serves society and when it harms it.
6) Tech change: environment, AI disruption, digital divide
Obligations of IT professionals regarding environment, automation, and digital equity.
INPUT OUTPUT
Input
Introduction Technological change continues to shape work and wealth. Discussion Topic Technological change is a constant in our society, and only accelerating. Today, and for the foreseeable future, societies, governments, companies, and public institutions (like hospitals and institutes of higher education) around the world will increase their reliance on and expectations of IT professionals. What obligations do IT professionals have to consider, and when considering: The environmental impacts of IT, especially data centre use and e-waste. Output
Technological change is not neutral. As IT professionals, we design and maintain systems that shape economies, labor markets, access to information, and environmental outcomes. Because of this influence, we have ethical obligations that go beyond technical competence. Our responsibility is not only to build efficient systems, but to consider their long-term social consequences. ## 1. Environmental Impacts: Data Centres and E-Waste IT professionals have an obligation to minimize environmental harm where possible. Data centres consume massive amounts of electricity and water, and hardware production contributes to carbon emissions and resource depletion. E-waste introduces toxic materials into ecosystems, often disproportionately affecting lower-income countries where disposal occurs. Our obligations include: - Designing energy-efficient software and systems. - Advocating for renewable-powered infrastructure. - Promoting hardware longevity and modular design. - Supporting responsible recycling practices. Even small design decisions (e.g., optimizing database queries, reducing unnecessary cloud computation) scale globally. Environmental responsibility should be treated as part of system quality, not an afterthought. ## 2. AI, Automation, and Economic Disruption AI and automation increase efficiency but also displace workers and reshape industries. IT professionals are often directly involved in building these systems. We are not solely responsible for economic policy, but we do have obligations to: - Consider foreseeable harms when deploying automation. - Avoid exaggerating capabilities or ignoring social costs. - Support retraining and upskilling initiatives. - Design AI systems transparently and ethically. The goal should not be to stop innovation, but to implement it responsibly. Automation that increases productivity while abandoning displaced workers creates inequality and social instability. Ethical professionalism requires acknowledging that technical success does not equal social success. ## 3. The Digital Divide Access to technology determines access to education, employment, healthcare, and civic participation. The digital divide affects rural communities, low-income households, seniors, and marginalized populations. IT professionals should: - Design accessible systems (usability, language, disability access). - Consider bandwidth limitations and device constraints. - Support inclusive hiring and training practices. - Recognize that not all work can be digitized, and avoid framing digital transformation as universally beneficial. Ignoring the digital divide risks reinforcing inequality rather than reducing it. ## Conclusion As reliance on technology increases, so does the responsibility of IT professionals. Our obligations include environmental stewardship, awareness of economic disruption, and commitment to digital equity. Professionalism in IT means recognizing that code has consequences — and that we are accountable not just for functionality, but for impact. Technological progress is inevitable. Ethical responsibility is a choice.
